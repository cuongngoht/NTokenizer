## Claims

1. **Delétang et al. show Chinchilla 70B, trained mostly on text, losslessly compresses ImageNet patches to 43.4% and LibriSpeech samples to 16.4% of raw size, beating PNG (58.5%) and FLAC (30.3%). This is a "raw" compression rate that ignores model size. Counting the parameters, the LLM is a far worse compressor (the paper's "adjusted" rate).** | "Language Modeling Is Compression" — https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/3cbf627fa24fb6cb576e04e689b9428b-Abstract-Conference.html (arXiv 2309.10668) | ICLR 2024 | 2023-09 (arXiv v1), 2024-05 (ICLR) | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high for the numbers; medium for the "adjusted rate" caveat (I know it from training data; the snippet only confirms "ratios ignore model parameter size") | seminal / theory
   - Correction for the article: the statement "LLMs compress images and audio better than PNG/FLAC" holds only if the cost of storing the model is excluded. The test also used small data chunks (I believe 2048 bytes each; not verified this run).

2. **Prediction equals compression: with arithmetic coding, a probabilistic model's log-loss equals its compressed code length. Delétang et al. use this equivalence to read scaling laws, tokenization and in-context learning as compression phenomena.** | same as #1 | ICLR 2024 | 2023-09 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high | theory

3. **Across about 30 public LLMs and 12 benchmarks, average downstream score is almost linearly correlated with compression efficiency (bits per character on held-out raw text). The reported Pearson r is about −0.95.** | Huang, Zhang, Shan, He, "Compression Represents Intelligence Linearly" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09937 | COLM 2024 (per arXiv v2 comment, snippet) | 2024-04 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed (COLM) | medium (exact model count, 30 vs 31, and r, −0.95 vs about −0.93 average, differ across snippets) | SOTA-empirical
   - Caveat: this is a correlation, and "intelligence" is defined as average benchmark score.

4. **Chinchilla was trained compute-optimally with 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, about 20 tokens per parameter. The "20 tokens/param" rule is derived from these figures and Approaches 1–2, not quoted as a law. The paper trained over 400 models (roughly 44M/70M to 16B params) on 5B to 500B tokens.** | Hoffmann et al., "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (arXiv 2203.15556) — seen via secondary pages, e.g. https://epoch.ai/publications/chinchilla-scaling-a-replication-attempt | NeurIPS 2022 | 2022-03 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high for 70B/1.4T; medium for "~20" as a rule | seminal

5. **The Epoch AI replication refit Chinchilla's Approach 3 from data reconstructed from the paper's figures. It obtained L = 1.8172 + 482.01/N^0.3478 + 2085.43/D^0.3658, versus Hoffmann's 1.6934 + 406.4/N^0.3392 + 410.7/D^0.2849. It found the original confidence intervals implausibly narrow. The refit agrees with Approaches 1–2, so it supports the roughly 20:1 ratio and does not overturn it.** | Besiroglu, Erdil, Barnett, You, "Chinchilla Scaling: A replication attempt" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102 ; https://epoch.ai/publications/chinchilla-scaling-a-replication-attempt | arXiv / Epoch AI | 2024-04 | accessed 2026-11-08 | preprint (peer review not verified) | medium-high | SOTA-empirical / debate

6. **Accounting for inference demand (about 1B requests or more), the optimal model is smaller and trained longer than Chinchilla-optimal. The authors found no saturation point even at very high token-to-parameter ratios. Modern over-trained models (far beyond 20 tokens/param) are therefore a deliberate choice, not a violation of scaling laws.** | Sardana, Portes, Doubov, Frankle, "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal: Accounting for Inference in Language Model Scaling Laws" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00448 | ICML 2024 (PMLR 235) | 2023-12 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high | SOTA-empirical

7. **Under data constraints, repeating data for up to about 4 epochs costs almost nothing in loss compared with fresh data. Returns then diminish toward zero. The paper is based on over 400 runs (10M–9B params, up to 1,500 epochs).** | Muennighoff et al., "Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models" — https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/9d89448b63ce1e2e8dc7af72c984c196-Abstract.html | NeurIPS 2023 | 2023-05 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high | SOTA-empirical

8. **Test-time compute scales too. A compute-optimal, per-prompt allocation of inference compute (verifier search or revision) is more than 4x more efficient than best-of-N. On problems where the small model has non-trivial success, it can beat a 14x larger model in a FLOPs-matched comparison. The benefit is concentrated on easy and medium prompts.** | Snell, Lee, Xu, Kumar, "Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally Can be More Effective than Scaling Parameters for Reasoning" — https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Abstract-Conference.html | ICLR 2025 | 2024-08 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high | SOTA-empirical

9. **Wei et al. define an emergent ability as one "not present in smaller models but present in larger models".** | "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682 ; https://openreview.net/forum?id=yzkSU5zdwD | TMLR 2022 | 2022-06 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high | seminal / debate

10. **Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo argue that apparent emergence comes from nonlinear or discontinuous metrics such as exact match: with linear or continuous metrics, improvement is smooth and predictable. They also induce fake "emergence" in vision models by choice of metric. The paper won a NeurIPS 2023 Outstanding (main-track) Paper award.** | "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?" — https://the-decoder.com/neurips-2023-these-are-the-top-papers-and-award-winners-at-the-largest-ai-conference/ ; https://www.twosigma.com/articles/neurips-2023-our-favorite-papers-on-llms-statistical-learning-and-more (arXiv 2304.15004) | NeurIPS 2023 | 2023-04 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high for the claim; medium for the award category (two secondary sources say main track, one wiki says D&B; the official NeurIPS blog was not checked) | debate

11. **Du, Zeng, Dong and Tang counter the "mirage" view. Measured against pre-training loss rather than parameter count, some tasks stay at chance until loss crosses a threshold, and this holds even with continuous metrics. They redefine emergence as an ability present only below a loss threshold.** | "Understanding Emergent Abilities of Language Models from the Loss Perspective" — https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/5f1eee2509599faeeb3570a887016a64-Abstract.html | NeurIPS 2024 | 2024-03 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed | high | debate
    - Synthesis for the article: the debate is not settled. "Emergence is just a metric artifact" overstates Schaeffer et al., and "abilities appear suddenly with size" overstates Wei et al. A fair framing is that loss falls smoothly, while some task metrics jump once loss passes a threshold.

12. **Malach proves that even linear next-token predictors, trained on chain-of-thought data, can efficiently approximate any function computable by a Turing machine. He introduces "length complexity", the number of intermediate CoT tokens needed. In experiments, a 775M attention-free MLP learns 4-digit multiplication with CoT.** | Malach, "Auto-Regressive Next-Token Predictors are Universal Learners" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06979 | ICML 2024 (venue from snippet only; not confirmed on PMLR) | 2023-09 | accessed 2026-11-08 | likely peer-reviewed (unverified) | medium | theory
    - Caveat: this is an expressivity and learnability result that depends on CoT supervision. It does not show that pre-training on web text achieves this.

13. **The counterpoint (Bachmann & Nagarajan): there are two phases, autoregressive inference and teacher-forced training. Teacher-forced training can fail to learn even a simple path-finding ("lookahead") task in-distribution, for both Transformers and Mamba, because of "Clever Hans" shortcuts from ground-truth prefixes. Multi-token / teacherless objectives partly fix this.** | "The Pitfalls of Next-Token Prediction" — https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963 | ICML 2024 (ICML-format text and an ICML 2024 paper mirror; also an ICLR 2024 AGI Workshop version) | 2024-03 | accessed 2026-11-08 | peer-reviewed (venue medium confidence) | medium-high | theory / debate
